Feed the Kraken.

A Kraken can turn battery power into a lot of motion. Your job is to get that power there cleanly, keep every connection tight, and stop extra current from becoming extra heat.

Part 1 / What is inside the can?

The controller travels with the motor

The Kraken X60 and smaller X44 are brushless motors with an integrated Talon FX controller and encoder. The power distribution board feeds the Kraken directly, while the CAN bus tells its onboard controller what to do.

Red and black carry energy. Yellow and green carry messages. Mixing up those jobs is a fast way to have a robot that either does nothing or releases the expensive smoke.

Two paths, two jobs. Battery → main breaker → power distribution → one protected branch → Kraken carries power. The roboRIO's CAN bus carries commands and telemetry.
Before plugging in the battery: inspect polarity end to end, put the robot on blocks, and have a second person check. Give every Kraken its own correctly protected power-distribution branch—never daisy-chain motor power from one Kraken to the next.
Part 2 / Share the source

Power distribution is a budget, not a buffet

A branch breaker protects the branch wiring; it does not promise the motor only ever sees the number printed on the breaker. FRC thermal breakers can pass higher current for short bursts. Several motors also add together at the battery, where total current causes voltage sag and can pull the whole robot toward a brownout.

Part 3 / Four wires, two jobs

Put each color exactly where it belongs

The two power wires feed the Talon FX inside the Kraken. The two CAN wires carry commands and telemetry. Keep the jobs separate: power goes from a protected PDH/PDP branch to the Kraken; CAN continues through the robot's CAN chain.

Power: use the red 10 AWG lead from the positive PDH/PDP output to the Kraken's positive power terminal, and the black lead from the negative output to the negative terminal. Use the CTRE preset torque driver with a T10 bit. Follow the terminal markings—never trust wire location alone.

Terminal habit: start every screw by hand, keep the ring terminal flat against the terminal, then let the preset torque driver click. Use the supplied-length terminal screw or the hardware documented for an approved adapter; a longer screw can damage the controller.
Part 4 / Wire is part of the circuit

A smaller gauge number means a bigger copper path

American Wire Gauge (AWG) runs backwards: 10 AWG is thicker than 12 AWG, which is thicker than 14 AWG. Thicker copper has less resistance. Less voltage is lost before it reaches the Kraken, and less power is cooked into the wire.

Kraken wire bundles use 10 AWG power leads to reduce resistance and voltage drop. Use wiring and protection permitted by the current FRC rules, make sound crimps, keep runs short, and add strain relief near the terminals.

Part 5 / Current becomes temperature

Torque is useful. Heat is the bill.

Near stall, a motor turns slowly while pulling enormous current. Very little electrical power becomes motion, so much more becomes heat in the windings and controller. A brief launch is one thing; holding a jammed intake, arm, or drivetrain against a wall keeps paying that heat bill every second.

P_copper heat produced in the motor windingsI_stator motor-side current that creates torqueR_winding electrical resistance of the motor windingsCTRE notes that stator limits restrict torque, reduce heat buildup, and can also reduce supply current during acceleration.
Why overheating is bad: hot copper has more resistance, so efficiency falls. Repeated or extreme heat ages insulation, bearings, magnets, solder joints, and electronics. The Talon FX can enter thermal cutoff, meaning lost motor output exactly when the robot is under stress. A current limit is cheaper than a cooked mechanism—or a dead match.
Part 6 / Pit checklist

Five ideas to walk away with

1. Give each Kraken a clean, protected branch from the power distribution board. 2. Use red/black 10 AWG power wiring where specified, keep it short, and make high-quality terminations. 3. Keep yellow/green CAN as a twisted pair, and use the CTRE preset driver with a T10 bit for power terminals or a T8 bit for CAN terminals. 4. Set current limits for the mechanism, not for bragging rights. 5. Log supply current, stator current, voltage, and temperature—heat is a symptom you can see coming.
