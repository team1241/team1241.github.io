Eight wires. One clean link.

A good Ethernet crimp is more than eight conductors in eight slots. Keep the pairs paired, keep the twists close, and make the plug grip the jacket—not eight fragile wires.

Part 1 / Where Ethernet goes

The robot network carries commands, video, and code

Your Ethernet cable connects devices on the robot’s network. The programming laptop uses that network to deploy code, open dashboards, and diagnose the robot. The radio bridges the driver-station side to the robot. The roboRIO runs robot code, while vision devices such as a Limelight send camera data and targeting results.

Typical logical path. If the radio does not provide enough Ethernet ports, place a small network switch on the robot and connect the radio, roboRIO, Limelight, and other Ethernet devices to it.

In the pit, you can plug the laptop directly into the radio or into the robot network for a wired connection. On the field, the laptop communicates through the field network and robot radio. Port layouts vary, so follow the documentation for the radio and switch your robot actually uses.

Do not confuse the plugs: Ethernet and some other modular connectors can look similar. Check labels before plugging in, route cables away from moving mechanisms, and add strain relief so a camera or radio port never becomes the cable anchor.
Part 2 / Know the target

Choose one standard, then repeat it

Cat 6 contains four twisted pairs. The plug is commonly called “RJ45,” though 8P8C modular plug is more precise. For a normal straight-through cable, terminate both ends as T568B–T568B or T568A–T568A. T568B is common in robot pits; matching the existing system matters more than the letter.

Part 3 / Prepare before you squeeze

Follow the cable from jacket to plug

Measure with service slack and make a square starting cut. Slide on any boot before working on the end. Strip only enough jacket to arrange the pairs, do not nick the conductor insulation, and keep the twists intact until you need to separate them.

Steps 6A and 6B are alternatives. Use whichever method gives you a straight, undamaged row while disturbing the least possible twist.

Look through the clear plug from the front and side. Confirm every color, conductor, and seating depth before crimping. Use a Cat 6 plug rated for the cable’s conductor type and outside diameter, then finish one full cycle with the correct ratcheting crimper.

Tug test: pull gently after crimping. The jacket should be captured by the plug. If eight individual conductors carry the load, cut the end off and try again.
Part 4 / Signal integrity

The twists are part of the circuit

Ethernet sends the voltage difference across each pair. This differential signaling helps shared noise cancel. Deliberate twist rates reduce crosstalk between pairs. Long untwisted tails, crushing, sharp kinks, or a split pair can pass continuity while corrupting high-speed data.

Try: increase untwisted length. Treat 13 mm (0.5 in) as a maximum, not a target; less disturbance is better.

Routing habits: avoid tight bends, staples, and crushing zip ties. Separate Ethernet from motors and high-current wiring where practical; cross noisy power wiring near 90° instead of running beside it.
Part 5 / Salvage the odd end

A mirrored mistake can work—but pairs still rule

The cable must pass two different checks. First, every conductor must arrive at the same pin number on the other end. Second, the two members of every physical twisted pair must land together on Ethernet’s pair positions: 1–2, 3–6, 4–5, and 7–8.

If the first plug has a wrong color order, you can sometimes salvage it by copying that exact order on the second plug. The new visual below shows the actual color in every contact. Matching columns means pin 1 reaches pin 1, pin 2 reaches pin 2, and so on—even when the color order is unusual.

Standard: both ends use T568B. Every color and pin matches, and all four twisted pairs stay together.

Best repair: if length allows, cut off the incorrect plug and re-terminate to T568A or T568B. Mirroring is a field salvage technique, not permission to split pairs. Label nonstandard cables and use a tester that detects split pairs; certification testing is the final word for Cat 6 performance.
Part 6 / Test before trust

Five ideas to walk away with

1. Match the standard at both ends. 2. Keep twists close to the contacts. 3. Seat all conductors and capture the jacket. 4. A mirrored mistake only works when required pairs remain paired. 5. Test the wire map, flex the ends, label the cable, then test the real link under load.
